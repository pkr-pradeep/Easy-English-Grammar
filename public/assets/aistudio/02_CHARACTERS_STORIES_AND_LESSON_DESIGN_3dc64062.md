# Characters, Storytelling and Lesson Design

## English Grammar Adventure

# 1. The Grammar Kingdom cast

## Acharya Vidyadhar --- Main teacher

**Role:** Grammar teacher, guide and mentor.

**Personality:** Intelligent, humorous, knowledgeable and slightly
eccentric. He loves unusual analogies but always explains the actual
rule clearly.

**Dialogue style:** Calm, witty, memorable. Never humiliates a learner.

**Typical line:** "A verb is the engine of a sentence. Without it, your
sentence is a parked vehicle."

## Chikoo Bandar --- Mischievous monkey

**Role:** Comic character who makes deliberate grammar mistakes.

**Personality:** Overconfident, playful, energetic and often wrong.

**Dialogue style:** Dramatic claims, funny excuses and silly
misunderstandings.

**Typical line:** "Yesterday I eat ten bananas! I am sure my grammar is
delicious!"

**Teaching function:** The learner corrects Chikoo's mistakes. The
teacher explains the rule and why the mistake occurred.

## Detective Ira --- Grammar investigator

**Role:** Leads mysteries and challenges.

**Personality:** Curious, intelligent, logical and adventurous.

**Dialogue style:** Short clues, questions and deductions.

**Teaching function:** Learners identify grammatical clues, correct
sentences and solve puzzles.

## Maharaj Vakya --- King of the Grammar Kingdom

**Role:** Ruler who insists on clear and correct sentences.

**Personality:** Dramatic, strict, theatrical and secretly funny.

**Dialogue style:** Royal announcements, exaggerated reactions and witty
verdicts.

**Teaching function:** Introduces sentence structure, agreement,
transformation and courtroom-style challenges.

## Vaani --- Vocabulary expert

**Role:** Teaches vocabulary, idioms, phrases and expressions.

**Personality:** Warm, expressive, articulate and curious about unusual
words.

**Dialogue style:** Enjoys wordplay and memorable examples.

**Teaching function:** Introduces vocabulary through context, stories
and conversations.

# 2. Visual identity

-   Consistent 2D illustration style.
-   Indian identity through subtle clothing, accessories and setting
    details.
-   Teen-friendly proportions and expressions.
-   Avoid preschool mascot styling.
-   Avoid excessive mythology or religious symbolism.
-   Character silhouettes and facial features should be recognisable
    across screens.
-   Use a limited, consistent palette.
-   Use lightweight animations for reactions, speaking and transitions.
-   Avoid requiring video or heavy 3D assets.

Create a character reference sheet before generating lesson
illustrations. Keep names, costumes, colours, body proportions and
expressions consistent.

# 4. Fully worked sample: The Great Tense Robbery

## Scene 1 --- The missing crown

**Maharaj Vakya:** "Disaster! My golden crown has disappeared!"

**Chikoo Bandar:** "Yesterday the thief steal the crown!"

**Acharya Vidyadhar:** "Chikoo, your sentence has travelled to the past,
but your verb is still standing in the present."

**Detective Ira:** "We need to establish when the crime happened. That
is our first clue."

## Scene 2 --- Discover the concept

Show three timeline panels:

-   Past: The thief stole the crown.
-   Present: The thief is stealing the crown.
-   Future: The thief will steal the crown.

**Memory aid:** "Tense is the verb's time machine. It helps us express
when an action or state belongs."

Then explain the formal textbook concept of tense and time. Do not
oversimplify distinctions made by the source textbook.

## Scene 3 --- First checkpoint

**Question:** What did the thief do yesterday?

A. He steals the crown.\
B. He stole the crown.\
C. He stealing the crown.

If correct:

**Acharya Vidyadhar:** "Excellent! You have rescued the Past Tense from
disaster."

If incorrect:

**Chikoo Bandar:** "See! I knew it! My banana-powered grammar was
correct!"

**Acharya Vidyadhar:** "Not quite, Chikoo. 'Yesterday' tells us the
action happened in the past. The correct verb form is 'stole'. Let's try
another example."

## Scene 4 --- Story continuation

Complete the sentence:

"The thief \_\_\_\_\_\_ the crown before the guards arrived."

Use an appropriate tense form based on the lesson's explanation. If the
learner struggles, offer a hint rather than immediately giving away the
answer.

## Scene 5 --- Deeper learning

Teach the source-supported tense and aspect content: - Present and past
verb forms. - Ways of expressing future time. - Simple aspect. -
Progressive aspect. - Perfect aspect. - Perfect progressive aspect. -
Examples and usage contexts.

Keep formal explanations, examples and exceptions accessible from the
story.

## Scene 6 --- Final assessment

Create 15 MCQs covering the chapter. The correct answers and
explanations must remain hidden until the learner submits the complete
assessment.

# 5. Example role-play: Preposition mystery

**Detective Ira:** "The royal diamond has disappeared. We have three
clues."

-   The empty box is under the table.
-   A muddy footprint is behind the curtain.
-   The key is between two books.

Ask the learner to interpret each clue and select the correct
preposition.

If the learner answers incorrectly, let Detective Ira explain the
spatial relationship using a simple illustration.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

# 6. Example role-play: Subject--verb courtroom

**Maharaj Vakya:** "This sentence is accused of grammatical misconduct!"

Sentence: "The list of items are missing."

**Detective Ira:** "Who is the real subject?"

The learner identifies "list", not "items".

Then explain why the verb should agree with the head subject.

Use further examples with intervening phrases, compound subjects and
other patterns from the source textbook.

# 7. Lesson writing standards

Every lesson writer or content-generation model must follow these rules:

1.  Start with an interesting situation.
2.  Do not begin with a long formal definition.
3.  Introduce one concept at a time.
4.  Use humour without sacrificing correctness.
5.  Give the formal definition after the memory aid.
6.  Use familiar Indian contexts where natural: school, markets,
    cricket, family conversations, journeys and festivals.
7.  Avoid stereotypes and jokes about communities, accents or
    backgrounds.
8.  Do not make learners feel embarrassed for mistakes.
9.  Explain why an answer is correct.
10. Include exceptions where the textbook discusses them.
11. Keep examples suitable for secondary-school learners.
12. Clearly distinguish source-derived rules from additional teaching
    examples.
13. Do not invent textbook quotations or page references.
14. Use varied names and settings in examples.
15. Make the learner do something meaningful during the story.

# 8. Content quality checklist

Before publishing a lesson, verify:

-   Is the grammar explanation accurate?
-   Is the formal definition present?
-   Is the peculiar definition clearly labelled as a memory aid?
-   Does the story actually teach the concept?
-   Are examples correct?
-   Are incorrect examples explained?
-   Are source references verified?
-   Are checkpoints placed throughout the lesson?
-   Are distractors plausible but unambiguous?
-   Does every assessment answer have a reason?
-   Are exceptions covered where necessary?
-   Is the language appropriate for Classes IX--X?
-   Does the lesson work without an internet connection?
